\section*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}

Urban bike-sharing systems (BSS) have emerged as pivotal green transportation infrastructures in contemporary smart cities, providing flexible, cost-effective, and zero-emission first-and-last-mile connectivity. However, modern dock-based bike-sharing networks suffer from severe structural inventory imbalances caused by tidal commuter flows. During peak morning and evening transit intervals, commuter surges exhaust available bicycles at residential docks (causing checkout stockouts) and saturate docking capacity at commercial and transit centers (causing return rejections). Effective fleet rebalancing requires accurate, station-specific, and hour-specific demand forecasting without forward-looking temporal data leakage.

This project designs and implements an end-to-end, production-ready machine learning framework and interactive decision-support application for hourly station-level departure demand forecasting across 70 docking stations in the San Francisco Bay Area Bike Share network. Using a foundational dataset of 669,959 validated historical trip transactions recorded over 733 calendar days (August 29, 2013 to August 31, 2015), the system formulates a continuous spatio-temporal matrix of 1,230,810 station-hour observations, explicitly capturing zero-demand hours to prevent dropout bias. A comprehensive feature engineering pipeline generates 54 strictly causal predictors encompassing trigonometric cyclical temporal representations, multi-scale historical demand lags (1h, 2h, 3h, 24h, 168h), strictly backward-looking rolling statistical aggregations (6h, 12h, 24h), and calendar indicators. An empirical leakage audit establishes zero target leakage and zero lookahead bias across all predictors.

To simulate real-world production deployment, models are trained and evaluated using a strict chronological split (70\% training: 861,560 rows; 15\% validation: 184,590 rows; 15\% unseen future testing: 184,660 rows). Three competitive ensemble regression algorithms---Random Forest Regressor, Tuned Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), and Categorical Boosting (CatBoost)---are independently trained and benchmarked. Tuned XGBoost achieves the best model performance on the out-of-sample test partition with the lowest Mean Absolute Error of 0.430550, Root Mean Squared Error of 0.898213, and a Coefficient of Determination ($R^2$) of 0.737418, explaining over 73.7\% of network-wide hourly demand variance.

The selected model is integrated into a production Streamlit web application powered by columnar Apache Parquet data caching, sub-second inference latency, station-level diurnal profile analysis, and an automated multi-station bike redistribution engine that computes net station balances and recommends transfer quantities from nearest safe donor stations while preserving a mandatory 3-bike safety buffer. The system demonstrates practical feasibility as an operational decision-support tool for urban transit dispatchers.

\section{Introduction}
\thispagestyle{fancy}

In contemporary urban planning and sustainable transportation engineering, municipal micro-mobility systems have undergone a profound paradigm shift. As rapid urbanization accelerates, metropolitan regions across the globe grapple with severe traffic congestion, deteriorating air quality, acoustic pollution, and elevated greenhouse gas emissions. In response, modern municipal administrations have prioritized active, shared, and green public transportation alternatives. Among these initiatives, urban bike-sharing systems (BSS) have emerged as one of the most transformative and environmentally sustainable mobility solutions, offering city dwellers on-demand, flexible point-to-point transit for short-to-medium distance trips while serving as a critical catalyst for first-mile and last-mile transit connectivity.

Despite their rapid global adoption, modern docked bike-sharing networks face severe operational and logistical vulnerabilities that threaten their service reliability. The primary challenge is the structural inventory imbalance caused by asymmetric commuter flows. During morning peak hours, tidal commuter volumes depart from residential areas and commuter rail terminals toward commercial districts, rapidly exhausting available bikes and precipitating stockouts. In the evening, the flow reverses, saturating destination docks and causing return rejections. Traditional reactive rebalancing relies on manual transport vans after stations run empty, which incurs severe traffic delays and operating expenses.

Advances in data-driven analytics have positioned machine learning (ML) as an effective solution for urban mobility forecasting. Machine learning models uncover complex, non-linear relationships across temporal cycles and commuter rhythms without requiring rigid physical assumptions. In particular, ensemble decision-tree regressors---such as Random Forest, Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), and Categorical Boosting (CatBoost)---are exceptionally well-suited for tabular mobility records due to their natural ability to capture threshold-based behaviors while maintaining strong resistance to overfitting and collinearity.

This project designs and implements an end-to-end machine learning framework and interactive operational decision-support tool to forecast hourly station-level bicycle departures across the 70-station San Francisco Bay Area Bike Share network. Using 669,959 historical trips across 733 days, the system builds a continuous Cartesian station-hour grid of 1,230,810 observations, explicitly populating zero-demand hours to eliminate selection bias. An audited pipeline generates 54 causal predictors, including cyclical encodings, multi-scale historical demand lags, and rolling statistics. Benchmarked under a strict chronological protocol, the selected Tuned XGBoost regressor achieves an out-of-sample MAE of 0.4305 and $R^2$ of 0.7374, and is deployed via a high-speed Streamlit application featuring automated Smart Dispatch rebalancing.

\section{Supporting Literature}

\subsection{Literature Review}
Forecasting urban bike-sharing demand has attracted widespread attention across the transportation informatics, operations research, and machine learning communities over the past decade. This section reviews three foundational peer-reviewed studies that directly informed the methodology, algorithm selection, and architectural design of this project.

\vspace{0.3cm}
\noindent \textbf{Paper 1 -- Ayd{\i}n C, Peker O, {\c{S}}en MA. Prediction bike-sharing demand with gradient boosting methods. \textit{Pamukkale University Journal of Engineering Sciences}. 2023;29(6):627--635.}

The paper titled ``Prediction bike-sharing demand with gradient boosting methods'' tackles the critical challenge of accurately forecasting hourly bike rental demand across diverse metropolitan environments by evaluating modern gradient boosting algorithms on two distinct international datasets: Konya (Turkey) and Washington, D.C.\ (USA) Capital Bikeshare. As municipal administrations shift toward sustainable micro-mobility, the authors emphasize that demand unpredictability and station inventory imbalances pose significant logistical challenges to operational viability. Reliable hourly forecasting is essential for optimizing fleet redistribution, reducing operating expenses, and preventing commuter service denial.

To model hourly demand, the authors utilize three competitive tree-based boosting architectures: Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), and Categorical Boosting (CatBoost). The feature engineering protocol incorporates temporal calendar indicators (hour of day, day of week, month, and seasonal indices) alongside normalized meteorological variables including temperature, relative humidity, and wind speed. The datasets encompass historical hourly transaction logs partitioned into chronological training and testing sets to evaluate generalization capability across diverse climatic conditions.

The empirical results proved that gradient boosting algorithms decisively outperformed classical statistical baselines such as ARIMA and multi-variable linear regression, achieving high predictive accuracy with $R^2 > 0.85$ on aggregate citywide counts. Notably, CatBoost and LightGBM exhibited superior computational training efficiency and exceptional resilience against noisy seasonal outliers. The authors highlighted CatBoost's ability to prevent overfitting through regularized tree growth and efficient categorical handling. However, the study evaluated primarily aggregate city-level hourly totals rather than granular station-level demand, and the authors recommended extending future modeling efforts to individual station micro-clusters.

\vspace{0.3cm}
\noindent \textbf{Paper 2 -- Karunanithi R, Chatasawapreeda P, Khan R. A predictive analytics approach for forecasting bike rental demand using machine learning algorithms. \textit{Decision Analytics Journal}. 2024;11:Article 100468, 1--14.}

This paper provides an in-depth comparative examination of forecasting hourly bike rental demand utilizing machine learning regression models on the Capital Bikeshare network, encompassing 17,379 hourly observations over two continuous years. The necessity for precise demand prediction is underscored as a crucial element for optimizing fleet availability, avoiding dock starvation during peak commute hours, and ensuring stable transit integration within urban transportation networks. The authors utilized a comprehensive feature set including calendar indicators (hour, weekday, holiday, season) alongside meteorological covariates (temperature, humidity, wind speed, weather situation).

Six regression models---Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) Linear Regression, Decision Tree (DT), Elastic Net, Random Forest (RF), Gradient Boosting Regressor (GBR), and Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) Artificial Neural Networks---were implemented and evaluated. Data preparation entailed missing value imputation, min-max feature normalization, and categorical encoding. Feature importance diagnostics identified hour of day, working day flag, and temperature as the most significant predictors of bike checkout volume.

The empirical findings demonstrated that ensemble tree algorithms decisively outperformed both classical linear models and deep neural networks on tabular mobility data. Specifically, Random Forest achieved $R^2 = 0.941$ and Gradient Boosting achieved $R^2 = 0.938$, whereas MLP achieved $R^2 = 0.862$ and OLS reached $R^2 = 0.389$. The authors proved that the piecewise constant approximations of decision trees naturally capture threshold-based mobility behaviors (such as sharp commuter rush hours) far more effectively than smooth gradient-based neural activations. However, the study utilized random train/test splitting rather than chronological time-series splitting, introducing potential temporal lookahead leakage, which highlights the imperative for rigorous chronological validation in operational systems.

\vspace{0.3cm}
\noindent \textbf{Paper 3 -- Yao H, Zhang Y, Wang Y, Zeng Q, Ye M. GSTGAT: Gated spatiotemporal graph attention network for traffic demand forecasting. \textit{IET Intelligent Transport Systems}. 2024;18(5):912--926.}

The study by Yao et al.\ (2024) is of direct relevance to this project because it explicitly utilizes the San Francisco Bay Area Bike Share network as a foundational real-world benchmark. The authors formulated docking stations as nodes in a dynamic topological graph and integrated Graph Attention Networks (GAT) with Gated Recurrent Units (GRU) to simultaneously capture spatial correlations across neighboring docks and temporal sequence dependencies over time.

GSTGAT achieved state-of-the-art predictive accuracy, reducing Mean Absolute Error by 8.4\% to 12.1\% compared to standard spatiotemporal baselines such as STGCN, Graph WaveNet, and DCRNN on the SF Bay Area dataset. Yao et al.\ proved that bike-sharing station demands are spatially interconnected; demand shocks at major transit interchanges (such as San Francisco Caltrain) rapidly propagate to nearby commercial stations. However, the authors acknowledged that deep graph neural architectures incur extreme computational training and inference latencies, requiring dedicated high-end GPU clusters. This renders them impractical for lightweight, sub-second web dashboard deployment, motivating our approach of capturing spatial and temporal dependencies through engineered lag features within highly optimized tree ensembles.

\subsection{Findings and Proposals}
The initial study reviewed by Ayd{\i}n et al.\ (2023) examined the prediction of hourly bike-sharing demand using modern gradient boosting algorithms on international metropolitan datasets. The authors evaluated Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), and Categorical Boosting (CatBoost). Their findings indicated that gradient boosting ensembles achieved superior predictive accuracy ($R^2 > 0.85$) while exhibiting exceptional computational efficiency compared to classical statistical and linear models. The primary takeaway from this research is that tree-based boosting methods naturally capture complex non-linear diurnal and weekly patterns without requiring arbitrary feature normalization, making them well-suited for practical transit redistribution.

The second study by Karunanithi et al.\ (2024) presented a comprehensive comparative benchmark of machine learning regression architectures for hourly rental demand on the Capital Bikeshare network. The study evaluated six regression architectures, including Random Forest (RF), Gradient Boosting Regressor (GBR), Decision Trees (DT), and Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) neural networks. The findings demonstrated that ensemble decision-tree regressors ($R^2 = 0.941$) heavily outperformed both artificial neural networks ($R^2 = 0.862$) and linear models ($R^2 = 0.389$). The critical insight from this work is that decision trees effectively capture sharp commuter rush-hour surges through piecewise constant splits; however, their reliance on random shuffle splitting highlighted the urgent methodological necessity for strict chronological time-series validation to prevent forward-looking data leakage.

The third research effort by Yao et al.\ (2024) evaluated deep spatiotemporal graph neural architectures (GSTGAT) on the San Francisco Bay Area Bike Share network. While the paper demonstrated that docking stations exhibit strong topological cross-station spatial diffusion, the authors acknowledged that deep graph neural networks incur prohibitive GPU memory overhead and multi-second inference latencies. The key conclusion is that while spatial cross-station interactions are vital, capturing spatial and temporal dependencies through engineered lag features and rolling statistics within lightweight tree ensembles strikes a far superior operational balance between accuracy, training speed, and real-time deployment feasibility.

The studies reviewed together support a comparative evaluation of Random Forest, Tuned XGBoost, and CatBoost for station-level hourly demand forecasting across the San Francisco Bay Area Bike Share network. Each algorithm presents distinct operational advantages: Random Forest provides robust variance reduction through bagging, XGBoost delivers state-of-the-art gradient optimization, and CatBoost prevents target shift through ordered boosting. The forthcoming work constructs a complete continuous Cartesian station-hour grid (1,230,810 observations) explicitly modeling zero-demand hours, engineers 54 causal lag and cyclical features audited against target leakage, and evaluates models under a strict chronological protocol using $R^2$, MAE, RMSE, and MAPE metrics. The selected model is integrated into an interactive Streamlit Graphical User Interface (GUI) with automated Smart Dispatch rebalancing, providing transit operators with real-time, sub-second operational decision support.

\subsubsection{Analysis of Feature Variables}

\noindent \textbf{a) Cyclical Feature Extraction and Encoding:}

Some numerical features in the dataset---hour (\texttt{hour}), day of the week (\texttt{weekday}), and month (\texttt{month})---exhibit cyclical or repetitive patterns. Using these as straightforward numeric values can mislead the model since values like 0 and 23 (for hour) or 1 and 12 (for month) may appear far apart numerically but are actually adjacent in operational time.

To accurately reflect these connections, each cyclic feature was transformed into sine and cosine components:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hour:} Encoded with sine and cosine functions based on a 24-hour cycle: $\sin(2\pi \cdot \text{hour} / 24)$ and $\cos(2\pi \cdot \text{hour} / 24)$.
    \item \textbf{Day of Week:} Encoded with a periodicity of 7 days: $\sin(2\pi \cdot \text{weekday} / 7)$ and $\cos(2\pi \cdot \text{weekday} / 7)$.
    \item \textbf{Month:} Encoded with a periodicity of 12 months to account for seasonal variations: $\sin(2\pi \cdot \text{month} / 12)$ and $\cos(2\pi \cdot \text{month} / 12)$.
\end{itemize}

This transformation preserves the cyclic characteristics of time and calendar data, enabling the machine learning models to recognize periodic patterns in bike rental demand without creating artificial boundary discontinuities.

\subsection{Analysis of Algorithms}

\subsubsection{Detailed study report of the proposed algorithms}

\noindent \textbf{Random Forest:}

Random Forest is an ensemble learning algorithm that constructs multiple decision trees during training and averages their outputs for regression tasks. Instead of relying on a single tree (which may overfit), Random Forest introduces randomness by selecting subsets of data and features for each tree, leading to more stable and accurate predictions.

\vspace{0.2cm}
\noindent \textbf{Strengths:} High prediction accuracy, reduces overfitting compared to a single decision tree, robust to noisy or missing data, works well with large datasets and many correlated features, and provides feature importance rankings for interpretability.

\vspace{0.2cm}
\noindent \textbf{Justification:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item In published benchmark literature, Random Forest consistently ranks among the top-performing algorithms for municipal mobility forecasting due to its robustness across tabular transit data.
    \item The bagging architecture naturally damps high variance arising from intermittent station checkouts without requiring complex regularization tuning.
    \item Compared to single decision trees, Random Forest prevents memorization of noise while preserving sharp non-linear boundaries.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.2cm}
\noindent \textbf{Process Flow:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Load the Dataset:} Import the processed Bay Area Bike Share dataset containing checkout volume (\texttt{demand}) and engineered spatio-temporal features.
    \item \textbf{Preprocessing:} Handle missing values, isolate multi-day rental outliers, and perform feature extraction to encode cyclic time patterns.
    \item \textbf{Train-Test Split:} Divide the dataset chronologically into training (70\%), validation (15\%), and testing (15\%) sets for model evaluation.
    \item \textbf{Bootstrap Sampling:} For each tree, Random Forest selects a random subset of training samples (with replacement) and random subsets of features.
    \item \textbf{Tree Construction:} Each decision tree is trained independently on its sampled data and feature subset, learning patterns in bike checkout demand variations.
    \item \textbf{Prediction Aggregation:} For regression, the final prediction is the average of all tree predictions, providing stability and reducing variance.
    \item \textbf{Model Evaluation:} Assess performance using metrics such as $R^2$, RMSE, and MAE on the unseen test dataset.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.64\textwidth, keepaspectratio]{diagram_3.pdf}
\caption{Flow chart for Random Forest}
\label{fig:rf_flow}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:rf_flow} illustrates the execution workflow of the Random Forest Regressor for hourly station demand forecasting. The step-by-step working of the algorithm proceeds as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Data Ingestion and Matrix Construction:} Ingest the 669,959 historical trip records and assemble the continuous Cartesian station-hour matrix (1,230,810 observations) with zero-demand filling and 54 engineered causal features.
    \item \textbf{Chronological Partitioning:} Partition the preprocessed dataset chronologically into a 70\% training set ($N = 861,560$ records), a 15\% validation set ($N = 184,590$ records), and a 15\% unseen test set ($N = 184,660$ records) to eliminate temporal lookahead leakage.
    \item \textbf{Bootstrap Sampling with Replacement:} Draw 200 independent bootstrap sample subsets with replacement from the 861,560 training records, allowing each decision tree to train on a slightly different subset of observations.
    \item \textbf{Random Feature Subsetting:} At each split within every tree, randomly evaluate a subset of $\sqrt{54} \approx 7$ candidate features ($\text{max\_features} = \text{`sqrt'}$) to de-correlate individual estimators and prevent dominant predictors from skewing the ensemble.
    \item \textbf{Individual Regression Tree Construction:} Grow 200 independent regression decision trees up to a maximum depth of 15 ($\text{max\_depth} = 15$) and enforce a minimum leaf node size of 2 samples ($\text{min\_samples\_leaf} = 2$) to limit tree complexity and prevent overfitting.
    \item \textbf{Prediction Aggregation by Arithmetic Averaging:} Combine the continuous demand estimates of all 200 trained decision trees by computing their unweighted arithmetic mean, substantially reducing prediction variance compared to a single decision tree.
    \item \textbf{Out-of-Sample Test Evaluation:} Evaluate the trained Random Forest ensemble on the sealed 15\% chronological test partition ($N = 184,660$) using Mean Absolute Error ($\text{MAE} = 0.4392$), Root Mean Squared Error ($\text{RMSE} = 0.9085$), and Coefficient of Determination ($R^2 = 0.7314$).
    \item \textbf{Operational Departure Demand Forecasting:} Input new station identifiers and temporal feature vectors to generate hourly bike departure predictions for transit dispatchers.
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{0.3cm}
\noindent \textbf{Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost):}

Extreme Gradient Boosting takes a sequential error-correcting approach rather than training trees independently in parallel. In gradient boosting, decision trees are built sequentially, where each new tree is specifically trained to predict and correct the residual errors made by the earlier trees, scaling each correction by a moderate learning rate to prevent overfitting.

\vspace{0.2cm}
\noindent \textbf{Strengths:} Exact second-order Taylor expansion loss optimization, built-in $L_1$ and $L_2$ regularization, cache-aware block structure for high-speed multi-threaded execution, and exceptional tabular regression accuracy.

\vspace{0.2cm}
\noindent \textbf{Process Flow:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Initialize Model:} Set baseline prediction to the overall training mean demand ($\bar{y} = 0.5443$).
    \item \textbf{Compute Gradients:} Calculate first-order gradients ($g_i$) and second-order Hessians ($h_i$) for each observation.
    \item \textbf{Build Regularized Tree:} Construct depth-10 regression trees optimizing split gain with $L_2$ regularization ($\lambda = 1.0$).
    \item \textbf{Shrinkage Update:} Scale tree predictions by learning rate ($\eta = 0.04$) and add to running ensemble.
    \item \textbf{Iterate:} Repeat across 600 boosting rounds with early stopping on the validation set.
    \item \textbf{Forecast:} Sum predictions across all trees to produce the final continuous demand forecast.
\end{itemize}

Figure~\ref{fig:xgb_flow} depicts the sequential error-correcting architecture of the XGBoost algorithm. The step-by-step working of the algorithm proceeds as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Data Ingestion and Feature Engineering:} Load the 669,959 raw trip transactions and construct the continuous station-hour matrix with 54 engineered temporal, cyclical, and historical lag features.
    \item \textbf{Model Initialization and Base Demand Prediction:} Initialize the XGBoost regressor and establish a uniform baseline prediction equal to the training set mean hourly demand ($\bar{y} = 0.5443$).
    \item \textbf{Gradient and Hessian Calculation:} Compute the first-order gradients ($g_i$) and second-order Hessians ($h_i$) of the mean squared error loss function for each observation to guide optimal tree splitting.
    \item \textbf{Regularized Tree Construction:} Build a depth-10 regression decision tree ($\text{max\_depth} = 10$) optimizing regularized split gain, incorporating an $L_2$ leaf penalty ($\lambda = 1.0$) to constrain model complexity.
    \item \textbf{Sequential Error Reduction and Shrinkage:} Calculate residual prediction errors from the existing model state and train subsequent trees on these residuals, scaling the contribution of each newly fitted tree by a shrinkage learning rate of 0.04 ($\eta = 0.04$).
    \item \textbf{Iterative Boosting (600 Boosting Rounds):} Repeat the gradient-guided tree construction sequentially across 600 boosting rounds, using the chronological validation set to track convergence and prevent overfitting.
    \item \textbf{Final Model Ensemble:} Aggregate the base prediction and the scaled outputs of all 600 trees to establish the final selected XGBoost model ensemble.
    \item \textbf{Out-of-Sample Test Evaluation:} Benchmark the ensemble on the sealed 15\% chronological test partition ($N = 184,660$) using Mean Absolute Error ($\text{MAE} = 0.4305$), Root Mean Squared Error ($\text{RMSE} = 0.8982$), and Coefficient of Determination ($R^2 = 0.7374$).
    \item \textbf{Hourly Demand Forecasting:} Generate continuous hourly station-level departure predictions ($\hat{y} = \max(0, \hat{y}_{\text{raw}})$) for operational fleet rebalancing and transit dispatch.
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{0.3cm}
\noindent \textbf{Categorical Boosting (CatBoost):}

CatBoost is an advanced gradient boosting framework designed to eliminate target leakage and prediction shift via ordered boosting and symmetric oblivious decision trees.

\vspace{0.2cm}
\noindent \textbf{Strengths:} Ordered boosting prevents target leakage, symmetric oblivious trees provide strong structural regularization, exceptional resistance to overfitting, and ultra-fast inference lookups.

\vspace{0.2cm}
\noindent \textbf{Process Flow:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ordered Setup:} Generate random permutations to establish chronological sequences for gradient estimation.
    \item \textbf{Unbiased Loss Calculation:} Compute gradients using models trained strictly on preceding samples in the permutation.
    \item \textbf{Oblivious Tree Building:} Construct depth-8 symmetric trees where all nodes at each level share identical split conditions ($2^8 = 256$ leaves).
    \item \textbf{Regularized Weight Estimation:} Optimize leaf values with $L_2$ regularization ($\lambda = 3.0$) and learning rate $\eta = 0.05$.
    \item \textbf{Additive Assembly:} Combine 600 oblivious trees into the final production ensemble.
\end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Model Initialization:} Initialize the CatBoost regressor configured with root mean squared error loss ($\text{loss\_function} = \text{`RMSE'}$) and 600 planned boosting iterations.
    \item \textbf{Ordered Boosting Setup:} Generate random permutations of the training dataset to establish an ordered sequence for gradient calculation, ensuring gradients are computed strictly from preceding observations to eliminate prediction shift and target leakage.

    \item \textbf{Regularized Leaf Weight Estimation:} Calculate prediction residuals on preceding samples and determine optimal leaf weights regularized by an $L_2$ penalty parameter ($\text{l2\_leaf\_reg} = 3.0$) to constrain extreme prediction shifts.
    \item \textbf{Iterative Boosting (600 Iterations):} Sequentially construct subsequent oblivious trees scaled by a shrinkage learning rate of 0.05 ($\eta = 0.05$) across 600 boosting iterations, with validation tracking to prevent overfitting.
    \item \textbf{Final Model Ensemble:} Formulate the final CatBoost ensemble as the additive sum of all 600 trained symmetric oblivious trees.
    \item \textbf{Out-of-Sample Test Evaluation:} Benchmark the ensemble on the unseen 15\% chronological test partition ($N = 184,660$) using Mean Absolute Error ($\text{MAE} = 0.4361$), Root Mean Squared Error ($\text{RMSE} = 0.8977$), and Coefficient of Determination ($R^2 = 0.7377$).
    \item \textbf{Hourly Departure Demand Prediction:} Generate continuous station-level bike departure forecasts for operational fleet monitoring and transit dispatch.
\end{enumerate}

Finally, the selected XGBoost model binary was serialized into \texttt{xgboost\_final\_model.pkl} and integrated into an interactive Streamlit web dashboard (\texttt{app.py}) to deliver sub-20ms demand forecasts alongside an automated Smart Dispatch redistribution engine that computes net station balances and recommends proactive bike transfers while preserving a mandatory 3-bike safety buffer.

\subsubsection{Implementation code}
The implementation code used in this project demonstrates a complete machine-learning workflow for predicting hourly station checkout demand using tree-based ensemble regressors. The process begins by importing essential Python libraries required for data handling, feature engineering, model training, and evaluation, including Pandas, NumPy, Scikit-learn, XGBoost, and CatBoost. The preprocessed dataset is loaded from Apache Parquet format to ensure rapid data access and low memory overhead.

Temporal features such as hour of the day, day of the week, and month are transformed into cyclic sine and cosine components to preserve periodic continuous patterns without artificial boundary discontinuities. Historical departure counts from previous hours ($t-1$, $t-2$, $t-3$) and previous days ($t-24$, $t-168$) are joined alongside strictly backward-looking rolling statistics (6-hour, 12-hour, and 24-hour means) to capture demand momentum. An empirical audit confirms that all predictors are strictly causal and contain zero forward-looking data leakage.

After preprocessing, the selected 54 input features are organized into the feature matrix $\mathbf{X}$, while the hourly departure count (\texttt{demand}) serves as the target variable $y$. The candidate models are then initialized and trained on the processed training records. Because decision tree ensembles perform recursive partitioning based solely on relative rank order, no artificial feature scaling, normalization, or standardization is required.

Once the models are fitted, out-of-sample predictions are generated for both the validation and dedicated future test sets. Performance metrics including Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), and the Coefficient of Determination ($R^2$) are computed. Raw model predictions are passed through a non-negative bounding function ($\hat{y} = \max(0, \hat{y}_{\text{raw}})$) to maintain physical validity. Feature importance rankings are extracted to identify the dominant drivers of rental volume. Finally, the selected XGBoost model binary is serialized using Pickle and Joblib as \texttt{xgboost\_final\_model.pkl}, enabling seamless integration with the Streamlit web dashboard.

\subsubsection{Model Planning (Training/ Testing split of the dataset)}
The model planning phase involved organizing the 1,230,810 station-hour records and determining an appropriate strategy for training and evaluating the regression models. In temporal mobility forecasting, the partitioning strategy between training and testing data is critical. Traditional cross-validation techniques (such as random K-fold shuffling) assume that individual records are independent and identically distributed ($i.i.d.$). However, time-series observations violate this assumption due to strong temporal autocorrelation. If random shuffling were applied to bike-sharing records, future observations from hour $t+1$ would be used to train models predicting hour $t$. The model would inadvertently learn from future signals that could never exist during real-world operational deployment, creating deceptively high test scores that fail in production.

To ensure scientific validity and simulate authentic operational deployment, this project strictly enforces a Chronological Partitioning Protocol. The continuous timeline spanning August 29, 2013 to August 31, 2015 is divided into three sequential, contiguous blocks:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Training Partition (70.0\% of observations):} Encompasses all records from August 29, 2013 at 09:00:00 through January 24, 2015 at 04:00:00 (a span of 17 continuous months, comprising 861,560 station-hour records). All feature statistics, distributions, and tree split thresholds are learned exclusively on this block.
    \item \textbf{Validation Partition (15.0\% of observations):} Encompasses all records from January 24, 2015 at 05:00:00 through May 14, 2015 at 01:00:00 (comprising 184,590 station-hour records). This block is utilized strictly for hyperparameter tuning, tree depth selection, and early stopping.
    \item \textbf{Unseen Test Partition (15.0\% of observations):} Encompasses the future chronological period from May 14, 2015 at 02:00:00 through August 31, 2015 at 23:00:00 (comprising 184,660 station-hour records). This partition was completely sealed and withheld during all feature engineering, hyperparameter search, and model fitting phases, serving as the definitive benchmark for out-of-sample operational evaluation.
\end{enumerate}

During the planning stage, the feature matrix $\mathbf{X}$ and the target variable $y$ were separated, and a fixed random state ($\text{random\_state} = 42$) was applied across all stochastic operations to guarantee complete reproducibility across experiments. The training phase combines multi-threaded parallel processing with tailored regularization strategies to optimize model accuracy while preventing overfitting. Each model architecture operates under a specific design rationale:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Random Forest Regressor:} Combines 200 independent regression decision trees trained on bootstrap samples with replacement. To prevent individual trees from memorizing localized noise, tree depth is restricted to a maximum depth of 15 ($\text{max\_depth} = 15$), terminal leaves are constrained to a minimum of 2 samples ($\text{min\_samples\_leaf} = 2$), and feature splits randomly evaluate a subset of $\sqrt{54} \approx 7$ features ($\text{max\_features} = \text{`sqrt'}$).     \item \textbf{Tuned XGBoost Regressor:} Employs sequential second-order gradient boosting across 600 rounds. The initial prediction is anchored at the training set mean demand ($\bar{y} = 0.5443$). Each subsequent tree is grown to a depth of 10 ($\text{max\_depth} = 10$) to capture complex non-linear commuter interactions, applying an $L_2$ leaf penalty ($\lambda = 1.0$) and a moderate shrinkage learning rate ($\eta = 0.04$). Subsampling 80\% of rows and features per tree introduces structural diversity. Training takes 386.06 seconds, delivering optimal gradient convergence.
    \item \textbf{CatBoost Regressor:} Utilizes symmetric oblivious decision trees of depth 8 ($\text{depth} = 8$, yielding $2^8 = 256$ leaves) across 600 iterations. CatBoost incorporates ordered boosting to prevent target leakage and applies an $L_2$ leaf penalty ($\text{l2\_leaf\_reg} = 3.0$) with a learning rate of 0.05 ($\eta = 0.05$). Its symmetric oblivious structure enables rapid bitwise histogram evaluations, achieving a fast training runtime of 35.13 seconds.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}
To evaluate model performance in a practical, operationally meaningful manner, three standard regression evaluation metrics are utilized:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Mean Absolute Error (MAE):} Measures the average absolute magnitude of errors between predicted bike demand ($\hat{y}_i$) and actual observed rentals ($y_i$), weighted equally across all observations:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{MAE} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} |y_i - \hat{y}_i|
    \end{equation}
    In this study, a test MAE of 0.430550 indicates that the model's hourly station forecasts deviate from ground truth by less than half a bicycle on average across the entire 70-station network.
    \item \textbf{Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE):} Calculates the square root of the average squared differences, placing a progressively heavier quadratic penalty on larger errors:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}
    \end{equation}
    An RMSE of 0.898213 confirms that even during busy commuter rush periods when demand surges, large forecasting deviations remain tightly bounded below one bicycle.
    \item \textbf{Coefficient of Determination ($R^2$):} Represents the proportion of total variance in hourly station departures that is explained by the independent feature set:
    \begin{equation}
        R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \bar{y})^2}
    \end{equation}
    An $R^2$ value of 0.737418 demonstrates that the model successfully accounts for over 73.74\% of all spatio-temporal demand variations on completely unseen future data using only historical lags and temporal features.
\end{enumerate}

Further operational analysis across temporal and spatial dimensions highlights key behavioral dynamics:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Diurnal Operating Windows:} Performance varies consistently across daily operating periods. During night quiescence (20:00--06:59), demand drops to a resting baseline of 0.18 bikes/hour, where the model achieves an exceptionally low MAE of 0.1842 and an RMSE of 0.4215. During the morning peak (07:00--09:59) and evening peak (16:00--19:59), heavy commuter tidal flows increase average demand and absolute error (MAE of 0.7421 and 0.8124, respectively). However, explained variance ($R^2$) peaks during these commuter rush hours at over 0.751, demonstrating that the model captures rush-hour dynamics with high fidelity.
    \item \textbf{Weekday vs.\ Weekend Commuter Dynamics:} On business weekdays (Monday through Friday), demand averages 0.6914 departures per station-hour with an MAE of 0.4982 and an $R^2$ of 0.7462. On weekends (Saturday and Sunday), demand drops significantly to an average of 0.1765 departures per station-hour, yielding a lower MAE of 0.2612 and an $R^2$ of 0.6895. The higher explained variance on weekdays confirms that structured commuting schedules are more predictable than spontaneous weekend leisure trips.
    \item \textbf{Spatial Stratification Across Station Tiers:} Activity is highly concentrated geographically. Major transit interchanges (Tier 1: San Francisco Caltrain, Ferry Building) handle high hourly volumes, where the model records an MAE of 1.4210 and an $R^2$ of 0.7684. Commercial core stations (Tier 2: Market at 4th, 2nd at Townsend) record an MAE of 0.6120 and an $R^2$ of 0.7240. Peripheral suburban stations (Tier 3: Palo Alto, Mountain View) record an MAE of 0.2215 and an $R^2$ of 0.6912. This spatial variation indicates that transit operators can achieve maximum efficiency by concentrating approximately 68\% of proactive rebalancing vehicle trips on the top 5 high-volume transit hubs.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Discuss your findings and conclusions}
The empirical results provide several actionable takeaways for municipal transit authorities:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Predictive Rebalancing is Feasible:} Achieving an overall test MAE of 0.4305 and bounding 89.24\% of forecast errors within $\pm 1$ bicycle proves that machine learning can accurately forecast hourly station departure requirements in advance.
    \item \textbf{Preemptive Lead Time for Redistribution Trucks:} By generating forecasts 1 to 3 hours ahead using strictly causal lags ($t-1$), transit dispatchers receive sufficient operational lead time to move redistribution trucks through city traffic before docks run completely empty.
    \item \textbf{Targeted Transit Hub Focus:} Spatial error decomposition confirms that rebalancing efforts should prioritize the top 5 transit hub stations (Tier 1), where hourly demand variance is highest, while peripheral suburban docks require only periodic routine monitoring.
    \item \textbf{Algorithmic Suitability:} Gradient boosted decision trees, particularly XGBoost and CatBoost, offer an ideal balance between predictive accuracy, rapid inference latency ($< 20$ ms), and minimal memory overhead compared to deep neural networks, making them well-suited for real-time edge deployment.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Model Deployment}

\subsection{Model implementation}
To translate machine learning research into an operational decision-support tool for urban transit dispatchers, the selected XGBoost model was integrated into a production web application built using the Streamlit framework.

The production architecture is engineered for speed, low memory overhead, and intuitive operational utility. The 1.23M station-hour dataset is stored using Apache Parquet format with Snappy compression, reducing disk storage from 420 MB (CSV) to 68 MB and accelerating cold-start loading times by over 800\%. The trained XGBoost model binary (\texttt{xgboost\_final\_model.pkl}) is serialized using joblib and cached in RAM upon application startup using \texttt{@st.cache\_resource}. Subsequent user queries bypass model deserialization, executing inference in under 20 milliseconds. The user interface is built using reactive components, providing real-time metric cards, interactive plotly charts, and dynamic rebalancing recommendation tables without page reloads.

When a transit operator queries the application, the backend executes a five-stage inference pipeline:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Parameter Acquisition:} The user selects an origin station ($s$), calendar date, operating hour ($t$), and inputs the station's current dock inventory ($B_{\text{curr}}$).
    \item \textbf{Causal Feature Retrieval:} The backend queries the indexed Parquet store to retrieve the strictly causal 54-feature vector $\mathbf{x}_{s, t}$ corresponding to $(s, t)$.
    \item \textbf{Model Execution:} The feature vector is passed to the cached XGBoost regressor, producing raw demand $\hat{y}_{\text{raw}} = f(\mathbf{x}_{s, t})$.
    \item \textbf{Non-Negative Bounding:} The raw output is passed through a rectifier: $\hat{y} = \max(0.0, \hat{y}_{\text{raw}})$, guaranteeing non-negative predictions.
    \item \textbf{Smart Dispatch Execution:} The predicted demand $\hat{y}$ and current inventory $B_{\text{curr}}$ are passed to the redistribution engine to generate rebalancing advice.
\end{enumerate}

The core operational contribution of the deployed application is the Smart Dispatch Recommendation Engine, which translates regression forecasts into vehicle rebalancing recommendations. To prevent stockouts from unexpected surges, the system maintains a mandatory safety buffer of 3 bicycles at every station.

The net balance is determined by comparing current inventory against expected rental demand plus the safety buffer. Based on this balance, stations are categorized into three practical operational states, as summarized in Table~\ref{tab:rebalance_rules}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Deficit State:} Available bicycles are insufficient to meet predicted demand plus the buffer. The system marks the station for replenishment and computes the exact inward transfer needed.
    \item \textbf{Surplus State:} The station holds extra inventory above predicted demand and safety needs. The system designates it as a safe donor station for outbound transfers.
    \item \textbf{Balanced State:} The station possesses adequate inventory to satisfy expected demand while preserving the safety buffer, requiring no truck intervention.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusions}

In conclusion, the Urban Bike-Sharing Demand Forecasting System effectively achieved its objective of accurately estimating station-level hourly bicycle departures and delivering an actionable decision-support platform for municipal transit operations. By ingesting 669,959 historical rental transactions from the San Francisco Bay Area Bike Share network and constructing a complete Cartesian station-hour grid of 1,230,810 observations across 733 days, the project resolved the pervasive problem of zero-demand exclusion bias that undermines many published studies.

A comprehensive feature engineering pipeline generated 54 strictly causal predictors encompassing trigonometric cyclical representations, multi-scale historical demand lags, and backward-looking rolling statistics. All engineered features were empirically audited to guarantee zero lookahead bias and zero target leakage. Using a rigorous chronological partitioning protocol (70\% train, 15\% validation, 15\% unseen future testing), three competitive ensemble architectures were trained and benchmarked. The Tuned XGBoost Regressor achieved the best model performance with a test Mean Absolute Error of 0.430550, Root Mean Squared Error of 0.898213, and a Coefficient of Determination ($R^2$) of 0.737418, explaining over 73.7\% of future demand variance. Furthermore, residual error analysis confirmed that 89.24\% of all test forecasts deviated by at most 1 bicycle from ground truth.

The trained selected model was integrated into a production-grade Streamlit web application powered by columnar Apache Parquet data caching, sub-second inference latency, interactive diurnal trend exploration, and an automated Smart Dispatch redistribution engine. By enforcing a mandatory 3-bike safety buffer, the system categorizes stations into deficit, surplus, and balanced states, automatically generating concrete transfer directives and identifying nearest donor stations to proactively prevent stockouts.

Overall, the project demonstrates the tremendous potential of data-driven machine learning methodologies for urban micro-mobility management. By combining a complete spatio-temporal matrix, strictly causal lag features, robust gradient boosting models, and an intuitive web interface, the system provides a scalable, computationally efficient, and practical solution for municipal bike-sharing rebalancing.

\section{Future Scope}

While the current system provides an accurate, robust, and computationally efficient demand forecasting framework, several promising avenues exist for future research and operational expansion:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Real-Time Telemetry and GBFS Streaming Feeds:} A primary extension is integrating live General Bikeshare Feed Specification (GBFS) data feeds. Incorporating real-time cellular dock telemetry (capturing instantaneous bicycle counts, available docking berths, and mechanical fault statuses) will allow the system to continuously update current inventory dynamically, enabling fully automated alert notifications for dispatch trucks.
    
    \item \textbf{Advanced Deep Spatio-Temporal Architectures:} While the current system utilizes gradient boosted decision trees, future iterations can explore hybrid deep geometric architectures such as Spatio-Temporal Graph Neural Networks (ST-GNN) and Temporal Fusion Transformers (TFT). Modeling physical station distances, road networks, and elevation topologies as graph adjacency matrices can capture non-Euclidean spatial demand diffusion across neighboring docks.
    
    \item \textbf{Dynamic Vehicle Routing Problem (VRP) Optimization:} Integrating an automated Vehicle Routing Problem with Time Windows (VRPTW) solver using Google OR-Tools will compute mathematically optimal turn-by-turn navigation routes for redistribution vans, minimizing diesel travel distance, driver hours, and vehicular carbon emissions.
    
    \item \textbf{Hyper-Local Micro-Climate Weather Integration:} Incorporating real-time meteorological API telemetry (including precipitation intensity, wind chill, temperature, and air quality indices) will enable the system to anticipate abrupt demand drops caused by sudden rainstorms, allowing dispatchers to cancel unnecessary rebalancing runs dynamically.
    
    \item \textbf{Scalable Cloud Deployment:} The platform can be containerized using Docker and deployed on cloud services such as AWS, Google Cloud, or Microsoft Azure to support multiple regional bike-sharing networks, higher query frequencies, and horizontal auto-scaling.
    
    \item \textbf{Mobile Application Integration:} Developing a dedicated mobile interface for redistribution truck drivers will provide turn-by-turn navigation, real-time pick-up and drop-off instructions, and immediate inventory status updates in the field.
    
    \item \textbf{Continuous Model Updating:} The system can incorporate automated retraining pipelines that periodically ingest newly recorded rental transactions and retrain model weights, ensuring adaptability to long-term demographic shifts, infrastructure developments, and seasonal changes.
\end{itemize}
