  • Target Variable: Hourly demand = number of bike trips starting from a station during an hour.

Data Preparation Stage 2: Grid Expansion (1,230,810 Rows)

Data Preprocessing: Solving the Zero-Demand Problem

SLIDE 06 / 15
STEP 1: HOURLY ROUNDING
Rounding Datetime to Hour
What is it?

2013-08-29 09:08:24 → 2013-08-29 09:00:00
2013-08-29 09:35:10 → 2013-08-29 09:00:00

Why do we use it?

Trip timestamps have arbitrary seconds. Rounding groups all trips that started in that specific hour.

Standardizes time to discrete 1-hour slots.
STEP 2: THE PROBLEM
The Zero-Demand Problem
What is the problem?

In raw ride logs, if nobody rents a bike at Station #12 at 3:00 AM, no row is recorded.

Why is this dangerous?

If we only train on hours with rides, the model never learns when demand is 0! It would severely over-predict nighttime demand.

Unrecorded quiet hours must be modeled.
STEP 3: COMPLETE TIMELINE
Grid Expansion (70 × 17,583)
Why multiply 70 × 17,583?

Stations exist even when 0 rides happen. To teach the model when demand is 0, we create a record for EVERY station at EVERY hour across 2 years:
70 stations × 17,583 hours = 1,230,810 records.

Zero-Filling:

Active hours get actual trip count; quiet hours get demand = 0.

Ensures continuous 24/7 citywide timeline.
STEP 4: EVENT CALENDAR
Adding Special Events
What is it?

Extracted city event and holiday dates from trip.csv into a calendar lookup dictionary.

Why do we use it?

Every newly added 0-demand station-hour row correctly inherits its holiday label (e.g. Halloween, Christmas).

Prevents missing calendar event metadata.
Stage 2 Summary Expanded 669,959 trips → 1,230,810 station-hour records (70 stations × 17,583 hours). Features: 3 base columns (station_id, hour_timestamp, demand). Quiet hours now correctly have demand = 0.

Creating Model Inputs Stage 4: Feature Extraction (1,230,810 Rows, 62 Features)

Feature Engineering (62 Candidate Features)

SLIDE 08 / 15
Illustrative Scenario (Example Context — not actual output): Predict demand for Tuesday, Oct 15, 2013 at 8:00 AM at Station #2 (San Jose Train Station). Below are the 5 feature families:
1. CALENDAR & FLAGS (14)
  • hour (0–23): Direct hour of day; captures commute spikes.
  • weekday (0–6): Separates Mon–Fri workdays from weekend leisure.
  • morning_peak (1): Flags 7:00–9:00 AM morning rush departures.
  • is_weekend (0/1): Binary flag isolating Saturday & Sunday behavior.
Captures daily commuter routines & peak hours.
2. CYCLICAL SINE/COS (8)
  • The Clock Gap: 23:00 and 00:00 are 1h apart, but numbers jump by 23!
  • hour_sin & cos: Maps 24h onto a continuous 360° circular face.
  • weekday_sin & cos: Connects Sunday smoothly back to Monday.
  • month_sin & cos: Connects December into January for seasons.
Preserves seamless continuous time without jumps.
3. INTERACTIONS (4)
  • weekday_hour: is_weekday × hour: gives weight to workday rush hours.
  • weekend_hour: is_weekend × hour: captures weekend midday curves.
  • month_hour: month × hour: adapts rush hour intensity by season.
  • peak_station: Flags rush hours specifically at transit hubs.
Separates busy Tuesday 8 AM from calm Sunday 8 AM.
4. PAST DEMAND LAGS (13)
  • lag_1h (12 trips): Departures in previous 1h (immediate momentum).
  • lag_2h (4 trips): Departures 2 hours ago (momentum direction).
  • lag_24h (14 trips): Departures yesterday at same hour (daily baseline).
  • lag_168h (15 trips): Departures same hour last week (weekly baseline).
Uses recent & recurring past trips as direct anchors.
5. ROLLING AVERAGES (15)
  • rolling_mean_3h (5.7): Moving average over past 3h; filters spikes.
  • rolling_mean_24h (2.1): Past full day's average hourly trip rate.
  • rolling_max_24h (16): Maximum single-hour surge recorded in last 24h.
  • lag_1h_ratio_24h (5.71): Surge ratio: station running hotter than normal.
Detects sudden real-time spikes above daily baseline.

Data Leakage Check Stage 5: Leakage Audit (62 → 54 Features)

Feature Audit: Removing Future Information

SLIDE 09 / 15
WHAT IS DATA LEAKAGE?

Data leakage happens when a feature accidentally uses information that would not be available in real life at prediction time.

Why Did We Remove 8 Features?
  • 1. Removed 3 Demand Deltas: Features like demand_change_1h used (current_demand - lag). But when making a prediction at 8:00 AM, we do not yet know the demand between 8:00 AM and 9:00 AM!
  • 2. Removed 5 Global Averages: Features like historical_mean averaged the entire 2-year dataset. That meant 2013 rows were using future numbers from 2015!
Audit Result: 62 features − 8 removed = 54 final causal features. All 54 features strictly use information available prior to the prediction timestamp.
Audited Feature Group Why It Leaked Information Real-Life Truth
3 Demand Deltas
demand_change_1h, etc.		 Used current hour's target answer in formula At 8:00 AM, you cannot know demand between 8:00 AM & 9:00 AM!
5 Global Averages
historical_mean, etc.		 Used future 2015 data to calculate averages for 2013 rows In 2013, you cannot see data from 2015!
Strict Lags Checked
lag_1h, 24h, 168h		 Verified lags only look strictly backward (t-1, t-24, t-168) Completely valid because past records already occurred.

Algorithms Used

Model Selection: How Each Model Works

SLIDE 11 / 15
MODEL 1: RANDOM FOREST
Random Forest (Bagging)
How does it work?

Builds many decision trees at the same time in parallel. Takes the average prediction across all trees.

Simple Example:

Tree 1: 14 • Tree 2: 16 • Tree 3: 15
Average = (14 + 16 + 15) / 3 = 15 trips

Why we use it?

Averaging many independent trees reduces errors and handles noisy data well.

Robust, parallel ensemble baseline.
MODEL 2: XGBOOST
XGBoost (Gradient Boosting)
How does it work?

Builds trees one after another in a line. Each new tree directly corrects the previous tree's residual mistake.

Simple Example:

Target = 15. Tree 1 predicts 11 (error: +4).
Tree 2 predicts the +4 error directly!

Why we use it?

Very strong at learning difficult non-linear patterns like morning and evening rush hour spikes.

Fast, high-precision sequential learner.
MODEL 3: CATBOOST
CatBoost (Ordered Boosting)
How does it work?

Builds trees sequentially like XGBoost. Uses ordered boosting to improve how the model learns from sequential data.

Why we use it?

Provides a state-of-the-art boosting benchmark and offers extremely fast prediction speeds during real-world inference.

Competitive accuracy with rapid inference speed.
